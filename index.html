About

I am a Data Analyst at NielsenIQ, a year into working with market research data for FMCG and financial services clients.

I came to data from a business degree, which is probably why I care less about the query itself than about what the number changes for the person reading it. Most of my day goes into making sure the data is right before anyone builds on it — and the moment a check starts repeating, I turn it into a script.

What keeps me in this field is the moment a messy dataset starts to make sense: a segment that behaves differently from the rest, a pattern that should not be there, a report that used to take a whole morning and now runs on its own. That is the part I genuinely love, and it is why I chose data over anything else.

Nine chapters, one brand. From a single tick to a decision.

FI-027 is assigned Ward 03, 90 interviews. The route and the sample frame land on the phone before the first door.

Not any household counts. The sample frame picks which doors qualify, and quotas by age and gender decide who inside the house can answer.

Coverage is never even. Ward 03 sits at 84% while a neighbouring ward runs ahead, and an uneven field changes how every downstream number reads.

Six interviews, 18.4 km and a timestamp on each one. The route is data too: time between doors, distance, and the order they were done in.

At the door the questionnaire opens: screener, quota check, then the grid. Interview 04 runs 21 minutes.

Every interview is recorded. The tool splits the audio into voice, silence and noise so a session can be judged without listening to all of it.

My Python engine takes the signals together: timestamp, location, duration, audio and the answers themselves, and scores the interview.

Against the configured thresholds one signal fails: silence at 34% where the rule is under 20%. Everything else passes.

A flag is not a verdict. It goes to review, then coaching, and the FI carries on with the next door. Repeat offenders change how work is allocated.
